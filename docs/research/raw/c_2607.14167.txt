### Research Report: Structured Feedback Improves Repair in an LLM Agent Loop

**1. Authors and Institution(s)**

*   Jaideep Ray, Independent Researcher
*   Ankit Goyal, Independent Researcher

**2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape**

The development of Large Language Model (LLM) agents that can iteratively interact with external environments, receive feedback, and refine their actions represents a significant area of research. Prior work has established the utility of iterative feedback mechanisms, exemplified by approaches such as ReAct, Reflexion, Self-Refine, and self-debugging methods, which enable LLM agents to learn from past failures and improve subsequent actions. Furthermore, the importance of purpose-built interfaces for coding agents and the benefits of execution-guided methods have been demonstrated in contexts like SWE-bench and SWE-agent, where LLMs engage with executable environments and respond to test messages.

This paper addresses a specific, yet critical, gap within this landscape: the precise nature and content of the feedback provided by an external validator to an LLM agent after a failed candidate action. While the value of iterative feedback loops is recognized, the interface defining *what exactly* a validator should communicate to the model for its next call remains largely underspecified. This research systematically investigates whether specific, structured information about a failureâbeyond a general rejection messageâcan enhance an LLM agent's ability to repair its actions, thereby contributing to the design principles for more effective and robust LLM agent systems. It aims to quantify the benefits of different types of feedback, distinguishing between raw diagnostics and information that explicitly identifies the failure location, observed value, and admissible alternatives.

**3. Key Objectives and Motivation**

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that LLM agents frequently fail on initial attempts and rely on subsequent retries guided by external validation. The effectiveness of these iterative refinement cycles is directly influenced by the quality and specificity of the feedback they receive. The authors hypothesize that generic rejection messages, while indicating failure, may not provide sufficient information for an LLM to effectively diagnose and correct its errors, leading to inefficient or unsuccessful repair attempts.

The paper aims to investigate this hypothesis through two specific research questions:

*   **RQ1: Does structured verifier feedback with expected alternatives improve repair over raw diagnostics under equal compute?** This question probes whether providing explicit information about the failure, including potential alternatives, can lead to a higher success rate for LLM agents within a fixed computational budget (represented by a call cap). The motivation here is to ascertain if more informative feedback translates into more successful repair strategies, rather than simply retrying with limited insight.
*   **RQ2: Is any gain caused by the keyed representation, the repair values, or both?** This question dissects the potential sources of improvement. It seeks to determine whether the observed benefits arise primarily from the *content* of the repair information (e.g., the specific details of location, observed value, and alternatives) or from the *format* in which it is presented (e.g., as a structured, keyed JSON record versus natural language prose). This distinction is important for guiding the design of validator interfaces and understanding how LLMs process different types of input.

To address these questions, the authors developed a controlled experimental framework, VeriHarness, designed to isolate the impact of different feedback policies. The underlying motivation is to move beyond empirical observations of iterative improvement to establish principled guidelines for constructing effective feedback mechanisms in LLM agent loops, thereby contributing to the development of more intelligent and self-correcting autonomous systems.

**4. Methodology and Approach**

The research employed VeriHarness, a custom code-controlled agent loop architecture, to conduct a controlled comparison of different feedback policies.

**VeriHarness Architecture:**
VeriHarness is designed to separate the concerns of candidate generation by the LLM from the acceptance and validation process, which is handled by external code.
*   **Control Plane:** Code, rather than natural language prompts, acts as the control plane, managing workflow state, enforcing budgets, and handling acceptance gates deterministically. This approach ensures that the experiment remains controlled and allows for replayability of tasks and failures across different policies. The LLM receives a compact task state and the current repair feedback, minimizing extraneous context.
*   **Orchestration:** A principal orchestrator builds a "compact state pack" and dispatches bounded LLM workers.
*   **Candidate Generation and Validation:** LLM workers generate structured candidates. External "gates" then check these candidates against schema, artifacts, and environment behavior. Only these gates can accept a candidate; the LLM's self-assessment is not sufficient.
*   **Data Persistence:** Every component of an LLM callâincluding the prompt, raw response, parsed output, gate result, model settings, and elapsed timeâis persisted, ensuring traceability and reproducibility.
*   **Failure Encoder:** A critical component is the "failure encoder," which maps gate-specific failure details into a common interface. For instance, in TextWorld tasks, an invalid action might trigger an encoder to expose a stable label, the command index where the failure occurred, the list of admissible actions in the current environment state, and the rejected action itself. "Oracle-blind evaluation" ensures that only visible, validated failures are used for feedback, excluding hidden post-hoc outcomes.

**Feedback Policies:**
Four distinct feedback policies were designed and compared, all receiving the same initial task state, generation instructions, gate, model, and call cap. Repair feedback is only provided after a failed candidate.
*   **RawDiag:** This baseline policy provides only the original validation error message, without any explicit repair fields or structured information.
*   **SameNL (Same Information in Natural Language):** This policy provides the failure location, the observed (rejected) value, and a list of expected admissible alternatives, all presented in natural language prose. It lacks typed labels or JSON keys.
*   **LocObs (Location and Observation):** This policy uses a stable failure label and named JSON fields to explicitly state the failure location and the observed (rejected) value. Crucially, it omits the list of expected admissible alternatives.
*   **TypedFields (Typed Repair Fields):** This policy provides the most comprehensive structured feedback, including a stable failure label and named JSON fields for the failure location, the observed value, *and* the expected admissible alternatives.

These policies facilitate specific comparisons:
*   SameNL vs. TypedFields: Isolates the impact of presentation format (prose vs. keyed JSON) while keeping the information content identical.
*   LocObs vs. TypedFields: Isolates the impact of including or omitting admissible alternatives, holding the keyed format constant.
*   RawDiag vs. SameNL: Quantifies the overall value of adding structured repair values (location, observed, alternatives) to a raw diagnostic.

**Primary Study Design:**
*   **Tasks:** 50 distinct TextWorld games were generated (seeds 20261051â20261100). Each game featured a three-room, four-object environment with a two-step quest. A "candidate" was defined as a JSON plan of up to four commands, executed from a fresh state. Success required achieving a terminal win. For invalid actions, the validator returned the command index and up to 12 admissible commands in TextWorld's deterministic order.
*   **Experimental Matrix:** The primary experiment comprised 50 games Ã 4 policies Ã 2 models, totaling 400 experimental rows.
*   **Models and Decoding:** Two LLMs were used: Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct-AWQ (served on an NVIDIA L4) and Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-AWQ-INT4 (served on a T4), both utilizing vLLM. Greedy decoding was employed with a 512-token output limit and a strict four-call cap per game.

**Robustness Checks:**
*   **Call Budgets:** A subset of 15 games was used to compare RawDiag and TypedFields across call budgets of 2, 4, 6, and 8.
*   **Sampled Decoding:** Qwen was tested on 20 games with temperature 0.3, top-p 0.9, and three inference seeds to assess the ordering under non-greedy decoding.
*   **HumanEval Scope Check:** 15 HumanEval tasks were used to identify conditions where repair feedback may *not* be effective. The validator exposed only one public assertion to the repair gate, while the full official test suite remained hidden for final scoring.

**Analysis:**
Game outcomes were paired by seed. Primary success differences were analyzed using 10,000 paired bootstrap samples and two-sided exact McNemar tests, with Holm correction applied to planned contrasts within each model. Sampled decoding analyses involved resampling 20 game clusters, and p-values used 100,000 game-level sign flips with Holm correction. For smaller samples (budget, HumanEval), raw counts and intervals were reported without relying on p-value thresholds as decisive. Prompt size was proxied by whitespace counts. The complete artifact, including source code, task rules, results, traces, and scripts, was made available for reproducibility.

**5. Main Findings and Results**

The research yielded several findings concerning the efficacy of structured feedback in LLM agent loops.

**5.1 Structured Feedback Improves TextWorld Success**
The primary experiment demonstrated that structured feedback significantly enhances terminal success rates in TextWorld games within a four-call budget.
*   **Qwen2.5-Coder-14B:** TypedFields improved terminal success by 44 percentage points over RawDiag (from 28% to 72% of games solved), with a 95% paired-bootstrap interval of 28â60 percentage points (Holm-adjusted exact *p* = 3.15Ã10â»âµ).
*   **Llama-3.1-8B:** Similarly, TypedFields improved terminal success by 42 percentage points over RawDiag (from 16% to 58%), with a 95% interval of 28â56 percentage points (*p* = 3.81Ã10â»â¶).
This improvement was consistent across both the coder-focused Qwen model and the general-purpose Llama model. A key observation was that most of this success difference emerged after initial retries, as first-call terminal wins were largely comparable across policies, indicating the feedback's role in facilitating successful repairs rather than initial correct generation.

**5.2 Specific Feedback Fields Matter Most**
Further ablations and comparisons pinpointed which components of the structured feedback contributed most to the observed gains.
*   **Role of Admissible Alternatives:** The inclusion of admissible alternatives proved to be a critical factor. LocObs, which provided location and observed value but *omitted* alternatives, performed close to the RawDiag baseline. In contrast, comparing TypedFields to LocObs (which isolates the presence of alternatives), success increased by 36 percentage points for Qwen and 40 percentage points for Llama. This suggests that explicit guidance on valid replacements is highly effective. The number of invalid-action episodes also drastically reduced when alternatives were provided (e.g., from 35 for RawDiag to 2 for SameNL in Qwen).
*   **Impact of Keyed Representation vs. Prose:** The study found no statistically significant advantage for a keyed JSON record and stable failure label over natural language prose containing the *same* repair values. SameNL (prose) improved over RawDiag by 42 percentage points for both models. The difference between TypedFields and SameNL was minimal (+2 points for Qwen, 0 for Llama), with both 95% intervals including zero and Holm-adjusted *p*-values of 1. This indicates that the information content of the repair values is more impactful than their syntactic presentation (prose vs. keyed JSON) for model reasoning.
*   **Call Efficiency:** While the success rates of SameNL and TypedFields were similar, TypedFields used marginally fewer calls for comparable terminal success (17 fewer calls for Qwen, 12 fewer for Llama). Average retry prompt sizes were similar across these policies, suggesting that the overall reduction in calls for TypedFields translated to lower total prompt word usage.

**5.3 Budgets and Sampled Decoding Robustness**
The findings maintained consistency under varied conditions.
*   **Call Budgets:** On a 15-game subset, the performance gap between TypedFields and RawDiag widened from budget 2 to 4. RawDiag's performance plateaued at 4/15 wins for Qwen and 2/15 for Llama from budget 4 through 8, showing no benefit from additional calls. Conversely, TypedFields continued to benefit from increased call budgets, particularly for Llama (reaching 60 points at budgets 6 and 8). This indicates that additional calls are more effective when accompanied by specific repair information.
*   **Sampled Decoding:** The ordering of policies (structured feedback outperforming raw, and no significant difference between keyed and prose with identical content) persisted when Qwen was tested with sampled decoding (temperature 0.3, top-p 0.9, multiple seeds).

**5.4 Conditions Where Feedback Cannot Help**
The HumanEval experiment served as a scope check rather than a general benchmark. For Qwen, the first candidate passed the single visible test assertion in all 15 tasks. Consequently, the VeriHarness never generated a repair opportunity, and all policies achieved 14/15 overall success, limited by one task where the visible test passed but a hidden test failed. This result underscores a fundamental limitation: structured feedback can only facilitate repair when the validator *detects and exposes* a useful failure; it cannot correct issues that are not observable or tested by the feedback mechanism.

**6. Significance and Potential Impact**

This research offers significant implications for the design and implementation of LLM-powered agent systems, particularly in scenarios requiring iterative refinement and self-correction.

**Implications for Validator Output Design:** The most significant finding is that validators should provide specific, detailed feedback following a failed candidate. This feedback should ideally include the precise **location** of the failure, the **observed value** that led to the failure, and crucially, a list of **admissible alternatives** when available. In practical applications, such fields could correspond to a file and line number, a failing assertion, an observed exception, or expected data types/behaviors. The study demonstrates that providing enumerative alternatives is particularly potent in guiding LLMs toward successful repairs, transforming a generic rejection into actionable guidance.

**Representation Format:** While named JSON fields may offer benefits for backend logging, routing, and integration with automated tooling, this study provides no evidence that the keyed format itself significantly improves the LLM's reasoning or repair capabilities over equally informative natural language prose. The effectiveness lies predominantly in the *content* of the repair information rather than its specific syntax. This suggests flexibility in implementation; developers can prioritize ease of parsing for models or human readability/tooling integration based on context.

**Efficiency of Agent Loops:** The results indicate that providing structured feedback makes additional LLM calls more productive. When agents receive only raw diagnostics, their performance plateaus quickly, suggesting they are unable to leverage further retries effectively. In contrast, agents receiving structured feedback continue to improve with increased call budgets. This implies a more efficient utilization of computational resources in iterative agent loops, as each retry is more likely to lead to a successful resolution. This is particularly relevant for cost-sensitive or time-constrained applications.

**Understanding LLM Repair Mechanisms:** The findings shed light on how LLMs process and utilize error information. The observed benefit of explicit alternatives suggests that LLMs can effectively integrate such lists into their generation process to select valid actions. The lack of a strong advantage for keyed JSON over prose indicates that current LLMs are capable of extracting and acting upon structured information embedded within natural language, reducing the strict necessity for rigid parsing requirements at the model input level.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The study acknowledges its focused scope, primarily utilizing TextWorld games and quantized models. Future research could extend these findings to more complex, real-world domains (e.g., repository-scale code repair, robotic control, scientific experiment design) and a broader range of LLM architectures and sizes. The HumanEval scope check highlights a critical boundary: the efficacy of feedback-driven repair is fundamentally limited by the validator's ability to detect and expose relevant failures. This underscores the need for robust and comprehensive validation systems that can provide diagnostically rich feedback across diverse failure modes.

In conclusion, this research provides empirical evidence supporting the design of "smarter" validators that return specific, actionable repair informationâespecially valid alternativesâto LLM agents. Such an approach can substantially enhance the agents' ability to self-correct, achieve higher success rates within given constraints, and contribute to the development of more resilient and autonomous AI systems.
