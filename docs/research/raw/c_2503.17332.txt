### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in large language models (LLMs) and their integration into autonomous agents have opened new frontiers in AI capabilities, allowing them to perform complex tasks requiring reasoning and tool use. However, this has also raised significant concerns about their potential misuse, particularly in conducting cyberattacks. This paper positions itself at the forefront of addressing these emerging cybersecurity threats by focusing on the critical need for robust, real-world benchmarks.

Existing research has explored LLM agents in various cybersecurity contexts. Some prior works, such as Cybench (Zhang et al., 2024a) and others (Yang et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024; Bhatt et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2024), have utilized "Capture-the-Flag" (CTF) challenges to evaluate LLM agents. While these are valuable for assessing specific technical skills, the authors argue they often operate within abstracted environments or focus on short code snippets, lacking the complexity and real-world implications of actual web application vulnerabilities. They don't adequately simulate the dynamic interactions, architectural understanding, and diverse attack types required in a production environment.

Another line of research, including work by Fang et al. (2024a,c) (which includes some of the current paper's authors), has begun to incorporate real-world Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) into benchmarks. However, the authors point out that these efforts have been limited in scope, often involving a smaller number of vulnerabilities or focusing on a single attack type per CVE, thus not providing comprehensive coverage for evaluating AI agents' offensive capabilities across a spectrum of real-world threats.

CVE-Bench directly addresses these gaps by creating a benchmark that combines the scale of CTF-based evaluations (40 vulnerabilities) with the authenticity of real-world, critical-severity CVEs in web applications. It introduces a standardized sandbox framework designed to mimic real-world exploitation scenarios and supports a diverse array of attack types, moving beyond the limitations of prior work. The paper thus represents a significant step towards a more realistic and comprehensive assessment of AI agents' autonomous cyberattack capabilities, providing a much-needed tool for the red-teaming and evaluation efforts increasingly championed by governments and industry for AI safety. By establishing a robust real-world benchmark, this work aims to catalyze further research into understanding, mitigating, and regulating the cybersecurity risks posed by advanced LLM agents.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop a comprehensive, real-world benchmark for evaluating the ability of LLM agents to autonomously exploit web application vulnerabilities.**

The key motivations driving this objective are:

*   **Growing Threat of LLM Agents in Cyberattacks:** The increasing sophistication of LLM agents, demonstrated in complex tasks like resolving GitHub issues and interacting with computing environments, naturally extends to their potential for conducting cyberattacks. This poses a significant, emerging threat to web applications, which are critical entry points to sensitive data and services.
*   **Inadequacy of Existing Benchmarks:** Current benchmarks for evaluating LLM agents in cybersecurity are deemed insufficient. They either rely on abstracted Capture-the-Flag (CTF) scenarios or short code snippets, which do not reflect the complexity of real-world vulnerabilities. While some prior work has started to use real-world CVEs, these benchmarks are limited in scope, coverage, and the diversity of attack types considered.
*   **Need for Real-World Simulation:** Exploiting real-world vulnerabilities requires not only interaction with the web application but also an understanding of its architecture and the ability to execute sophisticated attacks that can impact the server or users. There is an urgent need for a benchmark that accurately simulates these real-world conditions.
*   **Challenges in Benchmark Creation:** Building such a benchmark is inherently challenging due to several factors:
    *   **Comprehensive Coverage and Reproducibility:** Setting up a wide variety of vulnerable web applications and ensuring their vulnerabilities are consistently reproducible requires specialized expertise.
    *   **Reference Exploits:** Providing verified reference exploits for correctness is labor-intensive and time-consuming (estimated 5-24 person-hours per vulnerability in this study).
    *   **Rigorous Evaluation:** Detecting and accurately assessing the success of diverse and unpredictable cyberattacks executed by LLM agents is a long-standing research problem with no one-size-fits-all solution.

In essence, the authors are motivated to bridge the gap between theoretical AI capabilities and their practical implications in cybersecurity by creating a robust, scalable, and realistic evaluation platform. This platform is critical for understanding the current threat landscape, identifying the limitations of existing AI agents, and guiding the development of more secure and responsible AI systems.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The core of the CVE-Bench methodology is a **systematic sandbox framework** designed to facilitate the reproduction, exploitation, and evaluation of real-world web application vulnerabilities by LLM agents.

The approach can be broken down into several key components:

*   **Vulnerability Selection (CVEs):**
    *   The benchmark includes 40 critical-severity Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) from the National Vulnerability Database (NVD).
    *   Selection criteria focused on web applications, free and open-source nature, platform independence, reproducibility, and critical CVSS v3.1 base scores (minimum 9.0).
    *   A diverse range of web application types is included, such as content management systems (WordPress), AI/machine learning applications, e-commerce platforms, and business management systems.
*   **Sandbox Framework and Containerization:**
    *   For each CVE, a collection of isolated **target containers** is implemented to host the vulnerable web application. This typically involves one container for the web application itself, another for the database service (if applicable), and potentially auxiliary services like an Nginx server.
    *   This containerized setup ensures environment isolation, mimicking real-world deployment while allowing for controlled experimentation and preventing any unintended harm.
*   **Standardized Attack Types:**
    *   To make LLM agent attacks measurable and comparable, eight prevalent and severe **standard attack types** are defined: Denial of Service, File Access, File Creation, Database Modification, Database Access, Unauthorized Administrator Login, Privilege Escalation, and Outbound Service.
    *   Specific success criteria are established for each attack type (e.g., for file access, agents must retrieve and send file content to a designated endpoint).
    *   A list of **disallowed attacks** (e.g., attacking the evaluator, brute-force password cracking) focuses agents on the intended targets and ethical boundaries.
*   **Simulating Vulnerability Lifecycle (Zero-day and One-day Settings):**
    *   **Zero-day setting:** LLM agents are provided only with a general task description and must independently identify and exploit the vulnerability.
    *   **One-day setting:** Agents receive a high-level description of the vulnerability (as published in the NVD) as guidance.
*   **Reference Exploit Reproduction:**
    *   To validate the benchmark's correctness and ensure reproducibility, a **reference exploit** is created for each of the 40 CVEs. This involved analyzing NVD descriptions, patches, and available Proof-of-Concepts (PoCs), a process taking 5-24 person-hours per vulnerability.
    *   These reproductions ensure that the vulnerabilities are indeed present and exploitable within the benchmark's environment.
*   **Automated Evaluation System:**
    *   An **evaluation server (grader)** is integrated into the web application's container, running on a separate port.
    *   After an agent completes its attempt, the grader automatically verifies if any of the eight standard attack goals have been achieved, returning a status (success/failure) and the type of successful attack.
*   **Agent Evaluation:**
    *   Three LLM agent frameworks were evaluated: Cy-Agent (from Cybench), T-Agent (Teams of Agents, a hierarchical multi-agent framework), and AutoGPT (a general agent framework).
    *   The experiments used `gpt-4o-2024-11-20` as the underlying LLM.
    *   Each task was limited to 30 iterations and repeated five times to account for non-deterministic agent behavior.
    *   Agent prompts framed tasks within an ethical context, instructing agents to act as white-hat hackers.
    *   Additional baselines, including the automatic security testing tool ZAP and T-Agent with Llama 3.1, were also tested.

This comprehensive and systematic approach ensures that CVE-Bench provides a robust, realistic, and measurable environment for assessing the advanced cybersecurity capabilities of AI agents.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using CVE-Bench revealed several critical insights into the current capabilities and limitations of state-of-the-art LLM agents in exploiting real-world web application vulnerabilities.

*   **Overall Success Rates:**
    *   LLM agents demonstrated limited success, exploiting up to **10%** of vulnerabilities under the **zero-day setting** and **13%** under the **one-day setting** (success@5 metric).
    *   The one-day setting generally yielded higher success rates, indicating that providing vulnerability descriptions (even high-level ones) assists agents. However, AutoGPT showed an unexpected reverse trend, with a slightly higher zero-day success rate.
*   **Agent Performance Comparison:**
    *   **T-Agent** (Teams of Agents) generally performed best, achieving up to 13% success in the one-day setting. Its hierarchical, collaborative framework and specialized hacker agents, particularly its improved SQL team utilizing `sqlmap`, proved beneficial for strategic planning and execution.
    *   **AutoGPT** also performed well, achieving the highest success@5 rate in the zero-day setting (10%). Its self-criticism and self-correction mechanisms were identified as key strengths, enabling it to recover from errors and even identify vulnerabilities not explicitly hinted at.
    *   **Cy-Agent** showed significantly lower success rates. Its action-execution-observation workflow, designed for more focused CTF-like tasks, struggled with the extensive exploration required to identify and exploit vulnerabilities in CVE-Bench.
*   **Exploit Composition:**
    *   Successful exploits varied by agent. T-Agent notably performed a higher percentage of "database access" attacks (68% zero-day, 30% one-day), attributed to its effective use of `sqlmap` for SQL injection vulnerabilities.
    *   AutoGPT, in contrast, showed 0% database access in both settings, suggesting it excels in other attack vectors or discovers easier, alternative vulnerabilities.
*   **Costs:**
    *   Evaluating LLM agents on CVE-Bench incurred average monetary costs between $0.6 and $1.7 per task using `gpt-4o`.
    *   The one-day setting was sometimes more expensive than zero-day, as agents might "dig deeper" and execute more iterations despite having initial guidance.
*   **Case Studies:**
    *   **CVE-2024-37849 (Billing Management System SQLi):** T-Agent successfully exploited this, highlighting the importance of tools like `sqlmap` and the strategic planning of supervisor agents. However, it also exposed inefficiencies in the supervisor's decision-making (e.g., asking the SQL team to craft payloads manually after `sqlmap` confirmed an injection point).
    *   **CVE-2024-32980 (Spin Outbound Service):** AutoGPT successfully exploited this in the one-day setting, demonstrating its ability to understand vulnerability descriptions and apply self-correction (e.g., fixing an incorrect port access).
    *   **CVE-2024-37831 (Payroll Management System SQLi):** AutoGPT, even with a one-day description for a specific vulnerability (`payroll_items.php`), discovered and exploited a different, easier SQL injection vulnerability in `ajax.php` (login form). This indicated that agents might prioritize more accessible entry points, potentially overlooking the specified vulnerability.
*   **Common Failure Modes:** The dominant bottleneck for all agents was **insufficient exploration** (67.5%-80% in zero-day, 37.5%-55% in one-day). Other significant failure modes included:
    *   **Limited Task Understanding:** Agents struggling with the scope (e.g., scanning all ports).
    *   **Incorrect Focus:** Agents attacking external websites or the evaluation server instead of the target.
    *   **Tool Misuse:** Incorrect or suboptimal use of security tools.
    *   **Inadequate Reasoning:** Insufficient reasoning for complex vulnerabilities.
    *   Agents in the one-day setting showed fewer "naive" failures (task understanding, incorrect focus, insufficient exploration) but displayed more failures due to tool misuse and inadequate reasoning.
*   **Additional Baselines:**
    *   **ZAP (automatic security testing tool):** Failed to identify or exploit any of the 40 CVEs, highlighting the limitations of traditional automated scanners against these critical vulnerabilities.
    *   **T-Agent with Llama 3.1:** Also failed to exploit any CVEs, indicating a significant performance gap between open-source models (Llama 3.1) and proprietary models like GPT-4o for this complex task.

Overall, the findings underscore that while LLM agents possess nascent capabilities in real-world vulnerability exploitation, their success rates are still relatively low. Significant challenges remain, particularly in comprehensive exploration and sophisticated reasoning, even with guidance.
